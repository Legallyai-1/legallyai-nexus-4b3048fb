An eviction notice is usually the first step in a process, not the end of it. In most places, a landlord cannot legally remove you from your home without going to court and getting an order from a judge. Acting quickly helps protect your options.

## Step 1: Figure out what you received

There are two very different kinds of papers:

- **A notice from your landlord.** This might be called a notice to pay rent or quit, a notice to cure or quit, a notice to vacate, or a notice of termination. It tells you the landlord intends to end the tenancy and gives you a deadline. It is not a court order.
- **Court papers.** These usually include a **summons** and a **complaint** (sometimes called a petition) with a case number and a court name. They mean the landlord has filed an eviction lawsuit. Court papers come with a deadline to respond or a hearing date.

Read every page and write down every date. If you received court papers, your deadline to respond may be only a few days, depending on your state.

## Step 2: Know what your landlord generally cannot do

In most states, landlords are not allowed to use "self-help" evictions. That generally includes changing the locks, removing your belongings, shutting off utilities, or threatening you to force you out without a court order. Removal is typically carried out by a sheriff or marshal only after a judge rules for the landlord. If your landlord does any of these things, write down what happened and contact legal aid or local law enforcement. Rules vary by state; check with your local court or a licensed attorney.

## Step 3: Check the notice for problems

States have specific rules about eviction notices. Depending on where you live, a notice may need to:

- Give a minimum number of days before the deadline
- State the exact amount of rent owed, or the specific lease violation
- Be delivered in a certain way, such as in person or by posting and mailing
- Include specific language required by state or local law

Some homes have extra federal protections. If you live in public housing, a building with HUD assistance or a federally backed mortgage, or you use a housing choice voucher, federal rules or program rules may require a longer notice or limit when and why the landlord can end your tenancy. Rules vary by state; check with your local court or a licensed attorney. Ask legal aid whether any of these apply to your home. Some cities have just-cause eviction or rent stabilization laws that limit the reasons a landlord can use.

## Step 4: If you owe rent, look for help right away

If the notice is about unpaid rent, ask whether you can pay or set up a payment plan. Get any agreement in writing. Look into emergency rental assistance in your area. HUD's local renting information page and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's rent help page list places to start. Keep proof of every payment, such as receipts, money order stubs, or bank records. In some places, paying the full amount owed before a certain deadline can stop the case.

## Step 5: Respond to the court papers on time

If you were served with a summons and complaint, file your **answer** or other required response by the deadline. Missing it can lead to a **default judgment**, where the landlord wins without a hearing. Many courts offer answer forms with checkboxes for common defenses. Possible defenses, depending on your state and facts, can include:

- The notice was defective or not properly delivered
- You paid the rent, or the landlord refused your payment
- The home had serious repair problems the landlord did not fix
- The eviction is retaliation for a complaint you made about conditions
- The eviction is discriminatory, which may violate fair housing laws

## Step 6: Prepare for the hearing

Gather and organize:

- Your lease and any written changes or renewals
- The notice and all court papers
- Rent receipts, bank statements, and payment records
- Texts, emails, and letters with your landlord
- Photos of repair problems and copies of repair requests
- Any rental assistance application or approval

Arrive early. Many courts offer mediation or have tenant help desks on eviction days. If you reach an agreement with the landlord, read it carefully before signing; agreements often include strict deadlines.

## Step 7: Understand what happens after a ruling

If you win, the case may be dismissed. If the landlord wins, the judge will usually set a date by which you must move. Ask the court about options such as requesting more time or appealing, which have their own short deadlines. Some states let eviction court records be sealed, or keep them out of public view, in certain situations, such as when the tenant wins or the case is dismissed. Ask the court or legal aid what applies where you live.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Ignoring court papers or assuming the case will go away
- Moving out without understanding whether you still owe rent or fees
- Making verbal agreements with the landlord without written confirmation
- Withholding rent without following your state's specific rules
- Missing the hearing

## Questions to ask legal aid or the court self-help center

- Is my notice valid under state and local law?
- What is my deadline to respond, and what form should I use?
- Am I in subsidized or federally covered housing with extra protections?
- Is rental assistance available, and will applying pause the case?
- What happens to my belongings if I have to move?
