A **power of attorney** (often shortened to POA) is a legal document in which one person gives another person authority to act on their behalf. The person who signs it is usually called the **principal**. The person who receives the authority is called the **agent** or **attorney-in-fact**. Despite the name, the agent does not need to be a lawyer.

People use powers of attorney to plan ahead for illness or incapacity, to handle a single transaction while traveling, or to get help managing finances as they age. Done well, a POA can avoid the need for a court-supervised guardianship or conservatorship later.

## The main types

- **General (financial) power of attorney.** Gives the agent broad authority over financial matters, such as banking, paying bills, managing property, and handling taxes.
- **Limited or special power of attorney.** Gives authority for specific tasks or a set period, such as selling a car or closing on a house.
- **Durable power of attorney.** Stays in effect if the principal later becomes unable to make decisions. Without durable language, some POAs end when the principal loses capacity, which is often exactly when they are needed. In many states, including those that adopted the Uniform Power of Attorney Act, a financial POA is durable automatically unless the document says it ends when the principal becomes incapacitated. In other states the document must say it is durable. Check your state's rule.
- **Springing power of attorney.** Takes effect only when a specified event happens, usually the principal's incapacity, often confirmed by a doctor. Not every state allows springing POAs. Florida, for example, generally treats a new POA that takes effect only on a future event as ineffective, with limited exceptions such as some military POAs. Where springing POAs are allowed, proving the triggering event can cause delays.
- **Health care power of attorney.** Lets an agent make medical decisions if the principal cannot. It may be called a health care proxy or be part of an **advance directive**. In most states it is a separate document from a financial POA.

## What an agent can and cannot do

An agent's authority comes from the document and from state law. An agent is a **fiduciary**, which means they must act in the principal's best interest, not their own. In general, an agent should:

- Follow the principal's instructions and known wishes
- Keep the principal's money separate from their own
- Keep records and receipts of transactions
- Avoid conflicts of interest
- Act only within the powers the document grants

Some actions, such as making gifts, changing beneficiaries, or creating or changing trusts, often require specific written permission in the document. A POA generally cannot be used to make or change the principal's will, and all powers of attorney end when the principal dies. After death, an executor or personal representative handles the estate.

## How to create a power of attorney

1. **Decide what you need.** Financial, health care, or both? Broad or limited? Effective now or later?
2. **Choose your agent carefully.** Pick someone trustworthy, organized, and willing to serve. Consider naming a backup agent.
3. **Use a form that meets your state's requirements.** Many states have statutory forms. Many have adopted some version of the Uniform Power of Attorney Act, but details still vary.
4. **Sign correctly.** States differ on whether the document must be notarized, witnessed, or both. Real estate transactions may require the POA to be recorded with the county.
5. **Share copies.** Give copies to your agent, and tell your bank, doctors, and others who may need to rely on it.

The principal must have the mental **capacity** to understand what they are signing at the time they sign. If capacity is already in doubt, a POA may be challenged, and a guardianship may be the only option.

## Using a power of attorney

Banks and other institutions sometimes ask for additional documentation before accepting a POA, such as an agent's certification or their own forms. Some states have rules about when a bank or other third party must accept a valid POA. Rules vary by state; check with your local court or a licensed attorney. If you are an agent, sign documents in a way that shows you are acting for the principal, such as "Jane Doe, by John Doe, agent." Never sign only the principal's name.

## Changing or ending a power of attorney

A principal with capacity can usually **revoke** a POA at any time. Do it in writing, notify the agent, and send notice to banks and others who have a copy. A POA also typically ends when:

- The principal dies
- The document's stated end date or purpose is reached
- A court terminates it
- In many states, if the agent is the principal's spouse, the agent's authority ends automatically when either spouse files for divorce, annulment, or legal separation, unless the POA says otherwise. A named backup agent may then take over.

## Protecting against misuse

Financial exploitation by an agent is a real risk. To reduce it:

- Choose an agent with a track record of honesty
- Limit powers to what is needed
- Require the agent to share account statements with another trusted person
- Avoid granting gifting powers unless you specifically want them

If you suspect misuse, you can contact Adult Protective Services, local law enforcement, or the Eldercare Locator to find local help. The CFPB also publishes free guides for people managing someone else's money.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Waiting until capacity is already in question
- Using a generic form that does not meet your state's signing rules
- Assuming a financial POA covers health care decisions
- Not naming a backup agent
- Agents mixing the principal's money with their own

## Questions to ask an attorney or legal aid office

- What signing, witnessing, and notarization rules apply in my state?
- Should my POA be durable, springing, or effective immediately?
- Do I need separate financial and health care documents?
- What powers should be spelled out specifically?
- How do I revoke an older POA properly?
