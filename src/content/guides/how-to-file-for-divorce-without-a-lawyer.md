Many people handle their own divorce ("pro se" or self-represented), especially when both spouses agree on the major issues. It can save money, but you become responsible for the paperwork, deadlines, and procedures.

## Step 1: Check whether you can file in your state

Before anything else, confirm that your state's courts can hear your case. Most states have a **residency requirement**, meaning you or your spouse must have lived in the state, and sometimes in the county, for a minimum period before filing. The required time varies by state. If you recently moved, you may need to wait or file somewhere else.

You also need to know which **grounds** your state allows. Every state now offers some form of no-fault divorce, usually described as "irreconcilable differences" or an "irretrievable breakdown" of the marriage. Some states also allow fault-based grounds. In a no-fault case, you generally do not have to prove that either spouse did anything wrong.

## Step 2: Decide whether your divorce is uncontested

A divorce is usually called **uncontested** when both spouses agree on everything: how to divide property and debts, whether either spouse will pay support, and, if you have children, custody, parenting time, and child support.

If you disagree on major issues, the case is **contested**. You can still represent yourself, but contested cases involve more hearings, evidence, and negotiation.

Be especially careful if any of these apply: there is a history of domestic violence, one spouse controls the finances, there is a business, a pension, or real estate to divide, or one spouse lives in another state or country.

## Step 3: Get the right forms

Most state court systems publish free divorce forms and instructions online, and many courthouses have a self-help center where staff can explain the forms (though they cannot give legal advice). Common documents include:

- A **petition** or **complaint** for divorce (sometimes called dissolution of marriage)
- A **summons** that tells your spouse about the case and the deadline to respond
- **Financial disclosure** forms listing income, expenses, assets, and debts
- If you have children, forms about custody, parenting time, and child support
- A **settlement agreement** or **marital settlement agreement**, if you agree on terms
- A proposed **judgment** or **decree** for the judge to sign

## Step 4: File the paperwork and pay the fee

You file the petition with the clerk of the court in the proper county, in person, by mail, or through an e-filing system if your court offers one. There is usually a filing fee. If you cannot afford it, ask about a **fee waiver**. Most courts have a form that lets you request to have fees reduced or waived based on your income.

Keep a stamped copy of everything you file.

## Step 5: Serve your spouse

Your spouse must be formally notified of the case. This is called **service of process**. In most states you cannot hand the papers to your spouse yourself. Common options include a sheriff, a professional process server, or another adult who is not part of the case. Some states allow service by certified mail, and some let your spouse sign a form acknowledging receipt.

After service, the person who served the papers usually completes a **proof of service**, which you file with the court. If proof of service isn't filed, the case usually can't move forward.

## Step 6: Exchange financial information

Most states require both spouses to exchange financial disclosures, even when they agree on everything. Gather:

- Recent pay stubs and tax returns
- Bank, retirement, and investment statements
- Mortgage, loan, and credit card statements
- Titles for vehicles and deeds for real estate
- Information about health insurance and any pensions

Be complete and honest; hiding assets can lead to penalties or a reopened judgment.

## Step 7: Reach an agreement or prepare for court

**Retirement accounts often need an extra order.** To split an employer retirement plan such as a 401(k) or pension, you usually need a separate court order the plan approves, called a **qualified domestic relations order (QDRO)**. The divorce decree alone usually isn't enough. IRAs, and government or military retirement plans, follow different rules. Contact the plan administrator early, and consider having a professional prepare or review the order.

If you agree on everything, put the terms in a written settlement agreement that follows your state's format. Vague language, such as "we will split the furniture fairly," invites later disputes. Be specific about who keeps what, who pays which debts, and dates for any transfers.

If your spouse does not respond by the deadline, you may be able to ask for a **default** judgment. If you disagree, the court may require mediation, especially where children are involved, and may schedule hearings.

## Step 8: Finalize the divorce

Many states have a **waiting period** between filing or service and the earliest date the divorce can become final. Once requirements are met, you submit the final paperwork, and in some courts attend a short hearing. Your divorce is not final until the judge signs the judgment or decree. Get certified copies; you may need them to change your name, update accounts, or refinance.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Filing in the wrong county or before meeting residency rules
- Serving papers yourself where your state does not allow it
- Forgetting to file the proof of service
- Leaving out retirement accounts, debts, or tax issues in the agreement
- Not transferring titles, retirement funds, or accounts after the judgment
- Assuming the divorce is final before the judge signs

## Questions to ask the court self-help center or an attorney

- What residency requirement and waiting period apply in my state?
- Which forms does my county require, and in what order?
- What methods of service are allowed here?
- How are retirement accounts divided, and does that need a separate order?
- Can I get limited-scope help to review my agreement before filing?

If safety is a concern, the National Domestic Violence Hotline and your local court can explain protective order options.
