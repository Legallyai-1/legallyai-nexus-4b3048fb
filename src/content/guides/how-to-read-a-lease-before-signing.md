A lease is a contract. Once you sign it, you are generally bound by its terms for the full period, and so is your landlord. Many disputes about deposits, repairs, fees, and moving out early come down to what the lease says. Taking an hour to read it closely, and asking questions before you sign, is one of the most useful things a renter can do.

## Before you start

Ask for a complete copy of the lease, including every addendum, rule sheet, and disclosure, ahead of signing day. Read it somewhere quiet, not at the leasing desk. If you are given only a summary or a signature page, ask for the full document. Never sign a lease with blank spaces.

## The basics: who, where, how long

Confirm that:

- Every adult who will live in the home is named, and the landlord or management company is identified with a contact address
- The unit address, parking space, and storage areas are correct
- The **start and end dates** are right, and you know whether the lease is fixed-term or month-to-month
- If you have roommates, you understand whether each of you is responsible for the full rent if another person does not pay (often called joint and several liability)

## Money: rent, fees, and deposits

Look closely at:

- **Rent amount, due date, and accepted payment methods**, including any fee for paying online
- **Late fees** and when they begin; some states limit how much a landlord may charge
- **Security deposit** amount, where it will be held, and the conditions for getting it back; many states cap deposits and set deadlines for returning them with an itemized list of deductions
- **Other charges**, such as pet rent, parking, trash, amenity, or administrative fees
- **Utilities**: which ones you pay, which the landlord pays, and how shared utilities are split
- **Rent increases**: whether and how rent can change during the lease or at renewal

Add up the true monthly cost, not just the advertised rent.

## Repairs and condition

The lease should explain how to request repairs and how urgent problems are handled. In most states, landlords must keep rental homes in a safe, habitable condition regardless of what the lease says. Be cautious of language that makes you responsible for all repairs or appliances.

Before moving in, complete a **move-in inspection** checklist with the landlord if possible. Take dated photos and video of every room, including existing damage. Keep copies. This record can help if there is a dispute about your deposit when you move out.

## Rules about living there

Check rules on:

- **Landlord entry**: how much notice the landlord must give before entering; many states set a minimum
- **Guests**: limits on how long guests may stay
- **Pets**: whether they are allowed, deposits, and breed or size limits; note that assistance animals for people with disabilities are treated differently under fair housing law
- **Subletting and assignment**: whether you can rent to someone else if you need to leave
- **Alterations**: painting, mounting a TV, or changing locks
- **Renter's insurance**: whether it is required and the minimum coverage

## Ending or renewing the lease

Many problems arise at the end. Look for:

- **Automatic renewal** clauses and how much notice you must give to move out
- **Early termination** terms, including any fee and how much notice is required
- What happens if you must leave because of military orders. If you are in the military, or about to join: the federal Servicemembers Civil Relief Act generally lets you end a residential lease early, without a penalty for doing so, if you enter active duty or get orders for a permanent change of station or a deployment of at least 90 days. You must give the landlord written notice and a copy of your orders. For a monthly lease, the termination usually takes effect 30 days after the next rent due date. Your installation's legal assistance office can help.
- Whether some states let tenants end a lease early in specific situations, such as domestic violence

## Red flags

Be cautious if a lease:

- Asks you to waive your right to repairs, to a court hearing, or to your deposit
- Lets the landlord enter at any time without notice
- Holds you responsible for all damage regardless of cause
- Contains terms that differ from what you were told verbally

Some clauses like these may be unenforceable under your state's law, but it is better to address them before signing. If the landlord made a promise, such as a new appliance or a free month, get it in writing in the lease or a signed addendum.

## Discrimination and your rights

The federal Fair Housing Act prohibits housing discrimination based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status (such as having children under 18), and disability. Many states and cities protect additional groups. Rules vary by state; check with your local court or a licensed attorney. If you believe a landlord discriminated against you, you can file a complaint with HUD or your state or local fair housing agency.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Signing before seeing the full lease and all addenda
- Relying on verbal promises
- Skipping the move-in inspection and photos
- Not reading the notice requirements for moving out
- Not keeping a signed copy of the final lease

## Questions to ask before signing

- What is the total monthly cost, including all fees?
- How do I request repairs, and how fast are urgent issues handled?
- What notice do I need to give to move out or not renew?
- Can I sublet or end the lease early, and at what cost?
- Under what conditions is the deposit not fully returned?
