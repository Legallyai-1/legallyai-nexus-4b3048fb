Small claims court is designed so that ordinary people can resolve smaller money disputes without hiring a lawyer. Typical cases include an unreturned security deposit, an unpaid personal loan, a contractor who took payment and did not finish the job, or damage to your property. The process is simpler than regular civil court, but it still has rules.

## Step 1: Make sure small claims is the right place

Start by checking three things on your state or county court's website:

- **The dollar limit.** Every state caps how much you can ask for in small claims. Limits vary widely by state, and some states set different limits for individuals and businesses. If your claim is above the limit, you may be able to give up the extra amount to stay in small claims, or file in a different court.
- **The type of case.** Small claims courts usually handle requests for money. Many cannot order someone to do something, such as finish a repair, and some types of cases, like certain evictions or claims against government agencies, follow separate rules.
- **The deadline.** Every claim has a **statute of limitations**, a deadline for filing. It depends on the type of claim and the state. Waiting too long can end your case before it starts.

## Step 2: Try to resolve it first

Before filing, send the other party a clear, polite **demand letter**. Some courts require you to ask for payment before you sue. A good demand letter:

- States what happened, with dates
- Names the exact amount you want and how you calculated it
- Gives a reasonable deadline to pay or respond
- Says you intend to file in small claims court if it is not resolved

Keep a copy and proof that you sent it. The letter can also be useful evidence. Ask whether your court offers free mediation, too.

## Step 3: Identify the right defendant and court

You must sue the correct person or business, using the correct legal name. For a business, that may mean looking up its registered name with your state's secretary of state, rather than the name on its sign. Suing the wrong entity is a common and costly mistake.

You also need to file in the right location, usually where the defendant lives or does business, or where the problem happened. Your court's instructions will explain the venue rules.

## Step 4: File your claim

Fill out your court's claim form, sometimes called a statement of claim, plaintiff's claim, or complaint. Describe the dispute briefly and state the amount you want. Pay the filing fee, or ask about a fee waiver if you cannot afford it. The clerk will assign a case number and, in many courts, a hearing date.

## Step 5: Serve the other party

The defendant must be formally notified. Depending on your state, service may be done by the sheriff, a process server, certified mail sent by the clerk, or another adult who is not part of the case. In most places you cannot serve the papers yourself. After service, file the **proof of service** before the deadline your court sets. If the defendant is not properly served, the hearing may be postponed.

## Step 6: Prepare your evidence

Judges in small claims decide quickly, so organize your case so it is easy to follow. Bring at least three copies of everything (one for you, one for the judge, one for the other side):

- Contracts, leases, receipts, invoices, and estimates
- Photos or videos, printed if possible, with dates
- Texts, emails, and letters, including your demand letter
- Repair estimates from more than one source, if you are claiming damage
- A simple timeline of events on one page
- A short calculation showing how you arrived at the amount

If a witness saw what happened, ask them to come. Some courts accept signed written statements, but a live witness is usually more persuasive. If a witness will not come voluntarily, ask the clerk about a **subpoena**.

## Step 7: The hearing

Arrive early, dress neatly, and check in with the clerk. When it is your turn, speak to the judge, not the other party. A clear structure helps:

1. What the agreement or duty was
2. What the other party did or failed to do
3. How that caused your loss
4. How you calculated the amount

Stay calm and stick to facts. Let the judge ask questions. Do not interrupt, even if you disagree with what the other side says; you will usually get a chance to respond.

Some courts announce a decision at the hearing; others mail it later. Rules differ a lot. In some states, such as California and Michigan, lawyers generally can't represent you at the small claims hearing, and appeal rights are limited. In California, the person who filed the claim can't appeal a loss on that claim. Other states, such as Texas, allow lawyers and let either side appeal for a new trial. Check your court's rules before your hearing.

## Step 8: Collecting a judgment

Winning does not mean the court collects for you. If the other party does not pay voluntarily, you may need to use collection tools your state allows, such as wage garnishment or a bank levy, which usually require additional forms and fees. Ask your court's self-help center what options apply and what deadlines exist for collecting.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Suing the wrong person or business name
- Filing after the deadline or in the wrong county
- Asking for an amount you cannot document
- Bringing evidence only on your phone, with no printed copies
- Missing the hearing, which can lead to dismissal or a default judgment against you

## Questions to ask the court clerk or self-help center

- What is the small claims limit in this court?
- Which service methods are allowed, and what is the deadline to file proof of service?
- Can the defendant file a counterclaim against me?
- Is mediation available before the hearing?
- What are my options if I win and the defendant does not pay?
