When parents separate, the most important question is usually how they will share responsibility for their children. Many parents work this out together. When they cannot, a judge decides. Understanding what courts look at, and how the process works, can help you focus on what matters and avoid common missteps.

## Two kinds of custody

Most states separate custody into two parts, though the names differ:

- **Legal custody** is the right to make major decisions about a child's life, such as education, non-emergency health care, and religious upbringing. It can be **joint** (shared) or **sole** (one parent).
- **Physical custody** is where the child lives day to day. It can also be joint or sole. When one parent has primary physical custody, the other parent usually has **parenting time** or **visitation**.

Some states have replaced the word "custody" with terms like "parental responsibilities" or "decision-making," but the ideas are similar.

## The "best interests of the child" standard

In every state, the central question is what arrangement is in the **best interests of the child**. Each state lists its own factors, but courts commonly consider:

- The child's age, health, and needs
- Each parent's ability to provide care, stability, and a safe home
- The relationship between the child and each parent, and who has handled day-to-day care
- The child's connection to school, community, and siblings
- Each parent's willingness to support the child's relationship with the other parent
- Any history of domestic violence, abuse, neglect, or substance misuse
- The child's own wishes, depending on the child's age and maturity

How much weight a child's preference gets, and at what age, varies by state and by judge. Many states have special rules when there is a history of family violence, which can affect custody and require supervised visitation or other safety measures.

Many states encourage arrangements that keep both parents meaningfully involved, when that is safe and workable.

## Which state decides

If parents live in different states, a uniform law called the Uniform Child Custody Jurisdiction and Enforcement Act (UCCJEA) usually decides which state's courts can make custody orders. The law generally gives priority to the child's **home state**: where the child lived with a parent for the six months right before the case was filed, or since birth for a younger baby. A state that already made a custody order usually keeps control over changes to it. If a move is involved or planned, get legal guidance before filing or relocating. Rules vary by state; check with your local court or a licensed attorney.

## How the process usually works

1. **Filing.** One parent files a request for custody, either as part of a divorce or separation case or as a separate case if the parents were not married. Unmarried parents may first need to establish legal parentage.
2. **Temporary orders.** The court may set a temporary schedule while the case is pending.
3. **Mediation.** Many courts require parents to try mediation or attend a parenting class before a hearing. A mediator helps parents reach their own agreement and does not decide the case. Where there is a history of abuse, ask the court about exceptions or safety accommodations.
4. **Evaluation.** In contested cases, a judge may appoint a custody evaluator or a **guardian ad litem** to investigate and make recommendations.
5. **Hearing or trial.** If parents still disagree, each presents evidence and the judge decides.
6. **Custody order.** The final order sets out legal custody, physical custody, and the parenting schedule.

## Writing a parenting plan

Whether you agree or not, a detailed **parenting plan** helps. Strong plans cover:

- A regular weekly schedule, including exchange times and locations
- Holidays, school breaks, birthdays, and vacations
- How major decisions will be made and how disagreements will be handled
- How parents will communicate, such as a shared calendar or written messages
- Transportation and who pays for it
- Rules about relocation and notice before a move

Vague terms like "reasonable visitation" can work for parents who cooperate well but often cause conflict when they do not.

## How to prepare

- Keep a simple, factual log of parenting time, missed visits, and important events
- Save school, medical, and childcare records showing your involvement
- Keep communication with the other parent civil and in writing when possible
- Follow any temporary order exactly, even if you disagree with it
- Focus on your child's needs rather than criticism of the other parent

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Withholding the child from the other parent without a court order or safety reason
- Discussing the case with the child or asking the child to choose
- Posting about the other parent or the case on social media
- Moving with the child without following notice and court rules
- Agreeing to a schedule you cannot realistically keep

## Changing a custody order

Custody orders can usually be modified if circumstances change significantly, such as a move, a change in a parent's schedule, or a concern for the child's safety. Until a court changes the order, the existing one remains in effect. Child support is a related but separate issue with its own rules.

## Questions to ask an attorney or the self-help center

- What factors does my state list for best interests?
- Is mediation required here, and are there exceptions for safety concerns?
- Do I need to establish parentage first?
- How should a long-distance or relocation situation be handled?
- What forms do I need to request temporary orders?

If you or your children are in danger, call 911. The National Domestic Violence Hotline can help with safety planning.
